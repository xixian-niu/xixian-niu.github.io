Asymmetric Intellectual Property and Follow-on Innovation: Evidence from Drug Repurposing

Latest draft October 2026

Paper (PDF)

Theories of cumulative innovation treat an upstream patent as a gate. This assumes symmetric protection: follow-on innovators can protect their own inventions from imitation. I show that when they cannot, the upstream patent also acts as a shield, because imitating a licensed follow-on innovation infringes it. Longer upstream protection can then increase follow-on innovation. Drug repurposing exhibits this asymmetry. A molecule’s patent covers every use, but new-use rights are narrow and hard to enforce. Using new molecular entities approved by the FDA between 1985 and 2024, and instrumenting effective exclusivity with FDA review time, I estimate that one more year of exclusivity raises expected post-approval indications by about 34 percent. The marginal effect vanishes beyond roughly 15 years. Originators and entrants both respond positively, as the shield predicts. The results point to a failure in the protection of new uses, not a case for longer patents.

Publications

Do Policy Incentives Align with Pharmaceutical Innovation Challenges? A Retrospective Analysis of 42 Countries, 2000–2022

with S. Mermelstein, A. Gentilini, C. Yue, M. Shaikh, M. Dewatripont, H. Stevens, and M. Miraldo

Health Economics, forthcoming 2027

Abstract

We assemble the first systematic dataset of 1,078 pharmaceutical innovation policies across 42 countries and 163 diseases (2000–2022), decomposing each policy into underlying features to move beyond the conventional push–pull distinction. Push and pull incentives prove similarly allocated, both concentrating in scientifically difficult areas with heavy academic and small-firm involvement. At the feature level, however, allocation diverges: financial and risk-sharing incentives follow scientific difficulty, expedited regulatory pathways follow disease severity and treatment scarcity rather than total burden, and knowledge-pooling and access conditions remain narrowly used. Policy activity clusters in high-income settings and in cancers and rare diseases, leaving antimicrobial resistance and maternal and neonatal conditions persistently underserved.

Working papers

Unbundling Global Innovation Policies: Mechanism Effects, Spillovers, and the Equitable Allocation of Pharmaceutical R&D

with C. Yue, S. Mermelstein, M. Miraldo, H. Stevens, M. Shaikh, and M. Dewatripont

Abstract

Innovation policies bundle several incentive mechanisms with offsetting effects, so estimated policy-level effects identify no single margin. Decomposing 1,100 policy introductions into six mechanisms and linking them to 1.9 million R&D grants across 42 countries (2000–2021), we exploit within-policy variation in mechanism composition to separate own-country effects, cross-country spillovers, and allocation relative to disease burden. Revenue subsidies raise R&D but redirect it toward commercially lucrative diseases and high-income markets, widening the gap between private and social returns. Mechanisms that lower regulatory and information frictions yield larger and more persistent gains, concentrated where profit incentives are weakest, and generate positive spillovers across borders. Mandated IP-sharing lowers appropriability and depresses short-run entry. Success-contingent rewards dominate unconditional transfers, consistent with reduced moral hazard and better selection.

Patent Box Tax Relief and Orphan Drug Development

Abstract

Nine EU countries have extended preferential IP-box tax rates to orphan-drug income since 2015. Using difference-in-differences on a firm–country–year panel of 78,060 observations across 30 EMA member states (2000–2024), I find that adoption raises orphan-drug launches by roughly 75 percent. The effect is confined to large firms, with no response from SMEs and no increase in designations or registrations, indicating that the policy stimulates commercialization rather than innovation.

Cross-country pharmaceutical innovation policy database

2023 – 2026

Core contributor, Hi-Prix Work Package 7 (Horizon Europe), Brussels

Co-built the first systematic database of pharmaceutical innovation policies, harmonising heterogeneous national measures into a common taxonomy of incentive mechanisms, and linked it to disease-level R&D indicators from the preclinical stage to launch. I designed questionnaires and interviewed health technology assessment specialists from universities, agencies and companies to validate entries and close gaps in the data.
